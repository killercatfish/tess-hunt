Dear Professor Muirhead,

I'm a BU alum (BLS, 2006). I've taught high school math and computer science online since 2020, after more than a
decade in the classroom. For years I've been building a curriculum around data science through aquaponics,
cryptography and astronomy, with a home computing setup as its foundation, and this project grew out of it.

Since early October I've been running a transit search of TESS data on that hardware, and it turned up a 5.45-day,
about 1.1 Earth-radius candidate inside TOI-913 b's orbit (TIC 407126408, a K dwarf at 65 pc). A one-page summary is
attached.

Up front: I built this with a lot of help from an AI assistant (Claude), directed by me. I checked the key numbers by
hand, but no professional astronomer has checked the work. I also wasn't first. The open-source eumemic/exoplanets
search detected the signal two days before I did, and I credit them. What I've added is an independent detection and
vetting, which we've been comparing in public on their GitHub.

I'm not asking for help publishing. The pre-registered TESS Sector 114 test next spring is the real evidence, and I'll
publish that result whatever it shows. My question is simpler: looking at the summary, where would you push hardest?
The open scenario is a T = 17 star 27″ away, which would need a 23% eclipse. Our per-sector centroids only weakly
disfavour it, and the target sits at Dec −81°, so ruling it out takes a southern telescope.

If the signal holds up in Sector 114 (results due by about April 2027), I plan to fund rented time on a southern
telescope (iTelescope, Siding Spring, about $400 an attempt) for one transit window next spring. If that would make a
useful student project, I'd welcome a student's help planning the observation and reducing the data.

If there's someone better placed to look at this, I'd be grateful for a name. Thank you for reading.
